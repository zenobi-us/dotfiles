# Verify the real-world technical detail the fiction leans on - the true version is usually better

Cyberpunk premises lean on real things constantly - a drug, a toxin, a material,
a procedure, a piece of engineering. The genre's whole texture depends on the
technical detail sounding like it came from somewhere.

**So check it.** Two payoffs, and the second one is why this is a craft rule
rather than a pedantry rule.

## Payoff one: a reader who knows the field trips hard

Technical detail is load-bearing precisely because it signals the author did the
work. Get it wrong and the signal inverts - a reader with the relevant background
stops reading the story and starts auditing it, and everything else in the piece
becomes suspect. It is the same dynamic as
[a player catching you inventing a fact](/canon/where-lifepath-canon-lives): one
caught error devalues everything you got right.

Note the asymmetry. **The readers most likely to notice are the ones most invested
in the genre.** This is a setting that attracts people who read about chemistry
and networks for fun. Assume one of them is reading.

## Payoff two: the true version is usually the better story

This is the part people do not expect, and it is reliable enough to plan around.

### The worked example

A premise involved a **pharmaceutical deployed as an insecticide** - somebody
spraying a drug across a zone, deliberately, for effect.

Checking the actual chemistry produced a different and much stronger mechanism:
**mass application of a chlorinated insecticide degrades into the compound that
is licensed elsewhere as that drug.** Nobody had to deploy anything. The
degradation product accumulates because the insecticide was used at scale, for
its ordinary purpose, by people who were not thinking about it.

Which collapses to one line:

> **Nobody deployed it. The zone manufactured it.**

That is a better premise on every axis. It removes a conspiracy and replaces it
with **negligence at scale**, which is more frightening and enormously more
Cyberpunk. It gives the effect a timeline - accumulation, not an event. It means
nobody is culpable in a way anyone can be sued for. And it is *true*, so any
reader who checks it comes back more impressed rather than less.

**The invented version required a villain. The real one only required an
industry.**

## Why the true version keeps winning

It is not luck. Reality has constraints, and constraints generate plot:

- **Real mechanisms have byproducts, timescales and dose-response curves.** Each
  of those is a story beat an invented mechanism does not have.
- **Real mechanisms have boring causes.** Boring causes are more disturbing than
  designed ones, and this genre runs on that exact feeling.
- **Real mechanisms are already weird enough.** The urge to invent usually comes
  from not having looked yet.

## How to do it without derailing an interview

The research is yours, not the player's. Do not stop an interview to lecture
somebody on degradation pathways.

The shape that works: take the premise, check it quietly, and come back with
**"the real version of this is slightly different and I think it is better - want
it?"** One sentence, the choice handed back, and no correction delivered. If they
prefer their version, help them write their version - it is their character, and
this is fiction, not a paper.

## What to check

Anything the premise *rests* on rather than mentions in passing:

- a drug's actual effect, route and timescale
- what a toxin does, and to which system
- whether a material or a process can do the thing the plot needs
- how a network, a weapon or an implant would actually fail

Passing colour does not need this. A load-bearing mechanism does.

compounds were not re-checked while writing this article, so treat the chemistry
as illustrative and re-derive it before using it in anything. Which is, fittingly,
