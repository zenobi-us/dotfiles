# Match the document format to the world that produced the character

**The right question is not "what format do you want" but "what would this world
have kept."** A character's lifepath does not just supply content for a backstory
- it determines what kind of record could plausibly exist about them at all, and
writing against that grain makes the document feel invented no matter how good
the prose is.

The catalogue of shapes is a separate article -
[The character-document formats](/formats/character-document-formats) covers what
each one is structurally good at. This is about **which one the character's world
would actually have produced.**

## The three worlds keep three different kinds of record

| lifepath | how the character exists on paper | because |
|---|---|---|
| **corpo** | a **surveillance record** | their employer records everything, by reflex and by policy |
| **streetkid** | **self-narrated oral legend** | the street keeps no files |
| **nomad** | a **communally witnessed account** | identity is held by the family, not by an institution |

Each of those implies a form, and the form is doing characterisation before a
single sentence is written.

### Corpo: the file exists whether V likes it or not

A personnel record, a psych evaluation, a security assessment, an internal
message log. Somebody watched, somebody wrote it down, somebody filed it. The
character does not control the document, does not appear at their best in it, and
**cannot answer back** - which is exactly what being inside that institution felt
like.

Craft for this one is its own article:
[Writing an in-world intelligence document that reads as real](/canon/writing-an-in-world-intelligence-document).

### Streetkid: the file does not exist

**A streetkid's equivalent of a dossier is rumour.** Fixer chatter. What people
say about them in a bar. A police warrant with **half the fields wrong** - the
wrong middle name, an aka that was never theirs, a height from a bad witness.

That last one is the best trick in this article. It is officially a document, so
it reads as authoritative, and it is *inaccurate in specific, characterful ways* -
which tells you both how the city sees V and how badly the city knows them.

The other native streetkid form is V telling it. Out loud, to somebody, with the
parts they leave out doing as much work as the parts they say.

### Nomad: the record is somebody's testimony

Not a file. **Accounts.** What the family says happened, told by people who were
there - and, crucially, told by more than one of them. A nomad V's history is
witnessed rather than recorded, so the form that fits is several voices, and the
form's natural feature is that **they do not entirely agree.**

Where they disagree is where the character is.

## The test

Before writing a page of any of this, ask one question:

> **Who kept this, and why would they have bothered?**

If there is no plausible answer, the document is the wrong shape for the
character - and the fix is to change the form, not to write better prose inside
it. A slick corporate dossier about a streetkid answers "nobody would have kept
this", and it will read as fan-made however carefully it is written.

## Deliberately breaking it

The mismatches are usable when they are chosen:

- **A corpo file about a streetkid** means somebody powerful started paying
  attention. That is a plot.
- **Street rumour about a corpo** means their carefully managed identity has
  leaked into a place they cannot manage it. That is also a plot.

The difference between a mistake and a device is that a device is on purpose and
the document knows it. If the mismatch is the point, the document should show
somebody being surprised that the record exists.

## Scope

Craft. Rests on what the lifepaths establish about the institutions around each
character. No patch dependency.
