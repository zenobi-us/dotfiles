# The character-document formats, and what each one is structurally good at

**The format is usually the unlock.** "Write your backstory" is a blank page and
a performance anxiety. "Write the police file on yourself" is a game with rules,
and people who swear they cannot write will fill two pages of it.

This is the catalogue: what each shape *is*, what its structure does to the
writing, and how each one characteristically goes wrong. These are starting
shapes, not templates - a form with blanks in it turns an interview into
homework.

Only one of these is machine-read: a **play dossier** is the format a mod, an AI
companion or a roleplay framework can actually consume, because its graded dials
and explicit lines survive being parsed. The rest are for humans.

---

## Told to someone

V telling it, out loud, to a specific person in a specific place.

**Structurally good at:** unreliable narration. A person talking *chooses what to
leave out*, and what V hides from a friend is characterisation you cannot get any
other way. Also the format for people who freeze at "write about yourself" but
can talk.

**The unlock question:** *who are they telling, and why tonight?*

**Shape:** an opening that assumes shared knowledge (no throat-clearing); a story
that jumps to the interesting part; at least one place where V deflects; and
whatever the listener says back.

**How it fails:** it drifts into monologue. If the listener has not spoken in a
page, the format has quietly become something else.

---

## An in-world document

The game tells most of its own story this way - files, memos, intercepted
messages, ads. Easy to start because the form does the work.

**Structurally good at:** distance. It suits people who want space between
themselves and the character, people who like systems, and any V who lies -
because a document has an **author with motives**, and the author's bias is
free characterisation.

Which author is most of the effect:

| author | what it reveals |
|---|---|
| **NCPD file** | what V has been caught doing, and what the city thinks they are |
| **Corporate personnel record** | what an employer measured, and what it missed |
| **Fixer's notes** | what V is worth, and who they can be sent against |
| **Ripperdoc's chart** | what V has done to their body and what it cost |
| **A letter that was never sent** | everything the other formats cannot say |

**The unlock question:** *who wrote this, and what do they want?*

**How it fails:** documents flatten feeling. One handwritten margin note carries
more than three paragraphs of file prose.

---

## A braindance

The memory as a recording, with editor's annotations - what the wire caught
beyond the visuals, what is degraded, what has been cut.

**Structurally good at:** a single formative event rather than a life, and at
**justifying its own gaps** - BD degrades, and edits are made by someone. It is
the most Cyberpunk-native format there is.

**The unlock question:** *who edited it, and what did they take out?*

**Shape:** a timestamp; sensory layers (what V saw, felt, and the bits the editor
flags); an interruption where the recording fails.

**How it fails:** it is a gimmick if the edit does not mean anything. The cut has
to be a choice by someone with a reason.

---

## A play dossier

Not a story. A reference for **playing** the character consistently - how they
decide, what they will and will not do, where their limits sit.

**Structurally good at:** being used rather than read once. This is the format
for long campaigns, roleplay across many sessions, and feeding a character into
a mod or an AI companion that has to act like them.

A shape that works, adapted to whatever the player actually cares about:

- **Look** - only the details that come up in play
- **History** - short. A dossier is not a biography
- **How they decide** - the core of it. Two or three principles in the player's
  own words, each with the situation that proves it
- **Lines** - what they will not do, and what they will do that surprises people
- **Graded dials** - the messy human variables as levels rather than prose:
  drinking, drugs, violence, honesty, mercy. `none / low / medium / high` beats a
  paragraph because it survives being read quickly mid-scene
- **Factions** - who they work with, who they will not, and the price of each
- **Attachments** - who can make them act against their own interest

**The unlock question:** *what would surprise someone who thinks they know V?*

**How it fails:** dials are addictive and thirty of them is the normal end state.
Keep only the ones that would change a decision. **A dial that has never been
consulted should be deleted.**

## One scene

A single moment, written properly, instead of a chronology.

**Structurally good at:** starting. Most "I have no backstory" turns out to mean
"I have one image and no plot" - so write the image. The rest arrives later or
never, and never is fine.

**The unlock question:** *what is the moment you already picture?*

**How it fails:** it rarely does. If it finishes and there is appetite for more,
the next question is what happened the next morning.

---

## Interview transcript

Someone else asking V questions - a fixer vetting them, a corpo screening, a
ripperdoc taking a history, a cop with the recorder running.

**Structurally good at:** legitimising non-answers. "No comment" is a character
beat in a transcript and a hole anywhere else. It also keeps the shape of an
interview that is already happening.

**The unlock question:** *what question do they refuse to answer?*

---

## Obituary, written early

What gets said about V after. Written now, by someone specific.

**Structurally good at:** working out what a character is *for*. Deciding how it
ends tells you what the middle has to be. Grim, effective, and Night City's whole
thesis.

**The unlock question:** *who is sorry, and who is relieved?*

---

## Choosing between them

| the state they are in | the format |
|---|---|
| an image, no plot | **one scene** |
| can talk but not write | **told to someone** |
| wants to play the character, not read about them | **play dossier** |
| shy about their own writing | **an in-world document** |
| stuck on who V *is* | **obituary** |
| one big event has to matter | **braindance** |

Two is a legitimate answer. A scene plus a dossier covers both halves - the
feeling and the function - and neither has to be long.

## Scope

Nothing here depends on a game patch. It changes when an expansion adds lifepath
content, or when a mod changes the prologue and moves the anchors a backstory
hangs on.
