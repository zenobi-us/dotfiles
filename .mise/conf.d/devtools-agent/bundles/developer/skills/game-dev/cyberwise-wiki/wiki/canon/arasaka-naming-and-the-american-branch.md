# Arasaka names hardware after warlords, and its American branch is a tenancy rather than a partnership

Two heuristics. Both are useful the moment somebody - a player, or you - is about
to invent an Arasaka detail, because they tell you whether the invention sounds
like it came from the same shelf as the real ones.

**`draft` because these are patterns observed across the setting's naming and
organisational material, not statements quoted from a document.** They are good
enough to judge plausibility with. They are not good enough to assert.

## Arasaka hardware is named after warlords

Look along the Arasaka product line and the pattern is consistent: **historical
Japanese military figures**, applied to weapons and equipment.

That gives a cheap test. When a name turns up and the question is "is this
Arasaka, or did somebody make it up":

- **A warlord's name is plausible.** It fits the shelf.
- **An abstract, aspirational or English marketing word is not.** That is a
  different corporation's register.

It cuts both ways, which is the useful part. It also means an invented Arasaka
weapon *built to the convention* will read as real, and will therefore need
labelling as invented more carefully than a name that obviously is not.

**Do not run the test backwards into a claim.** "That sounds like the convention"
establishes plausibility, never existence. A name that passes still has to be
found in the game before it is stated as canon - see
[A weapon can exist only in a shard](/canon/a-weapon-can-exist-only-in-a-shard),
which is exactly the case where a real, correctly-named Arasaka weapon turns out
not to exist as an object.

## The US foothold is recent and precarious

The other half is organisational, and it changes how an Arasaka character's
career reads.

**Arasaka's presence in the United States is recent, and it is not secure.**
Tokyo's posture toward the American branches is that they are territory to
operate *in* - not a partner, not an equal arm of the company. The relationship is
occupation-shaped rather than federated.

What that buys you when writing a corpo character:

- **Position in Night City is not position in Arasaka.** Seniority in the
  American operation does not translate upward.
- **Internal politics have a permanent axis**: the local branch against Tokyo's
  view of it. That is real friction available to any corpo backstory without
  inventing anything.
- **It explains institutional insecurity.** People in a branch that head office
  regards as a holding are people with reasons to act badly, and that is a motive
  generator that costs nothing.

It also frames the prologue: an American branch under scrutiny is exactly the
environment in which
[a failed assassination becomes a counterintelligence purge](/canon/the-corpo-prologue)
rather than a quiet internal matter.

## What not to do with either of these

**Do not use a plausibility heuristic to generate org-chart detail.** Named
executives, named internal departments, named projects and named AI systems are
where invented Arasaka lore gets mistaken for canon fastest, because the format
carries so much authority. If it is not in the game, it does not go in a document
without the player's name on it.

## Scope

Observed patterns across the setting's naming and organisational material, not
traced to a quotable source. Both want a citation before promotion past `draft`.
Neither is patch-dependent.
