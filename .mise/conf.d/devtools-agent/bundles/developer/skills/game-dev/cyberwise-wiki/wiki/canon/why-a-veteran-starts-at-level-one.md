# The diegetic answer to "why does my veteran start at level 1" is that the prologue is an amputation

It is the most common question in character work and it has a real answer.

Someone writes a V with fifteen years behind them - a corporate operator, a
career solo, a nomad who has fixed everything on four wheels twice - and then
starts the game unable to win a fistfight in Watson. The usual responses are bad:
retcon the backstory smaller, or shrug and say gameplay and story are separate.

**Neither is necessary. The prologue already does the work.**

## The prologue removes something, in every lifepath

The weakness *is* an amputation. V did not become incompetent; V lost the
apparatus that made the competence usable.

**Corpo V** is the clearest case, because the loss is enumerable:

- **Access.** Doors, systems, buildings, databases. Gone at the moment of firing.
- **Accounts.** The money was the company's.
- **Support infrastructure.** Intel, backup, transport, medical, legal, the
  ability to make a problem somebody else's. All of it belonged to the badge.

Corpo V's skill was never personal. It was **institutional**, and the institution
took it back. Everything that survives is the part V carries in their own body,
and that part is genuinely level 1 in Night City's terms.

**The other two need the same treatment done deliberately**, because their
prologues state the loss less explicitly:

| lifepath | what the amputation can be |
|---|---|
| **corpo** | access, accounts, infrastructure - stated by the prologue |
| **streetkid** | confiscation, a burnt reputation, a fixer who no longer takes the call |
| **nomad** | exile, and the loss of everyone who used to be the backup |

Any lifepath-appropriate **confiscation, exile or injury** does the same job. The
requirement is only that it be specific and that it be a *removal*, not a
reduction of the character.

## Why this is better than shrinking the backstory

Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters.

1. **It preserves the character the player actually wanted.** They came with a
   veteran. They keep the veteran.
2. **It explains the arc.** The game is about rebuilding the apparatus - contacts,
   money, chrome, a reputation. That is exactly what levelling is, in fiction.
3. **It generates play.** "What did V lose that they are still trying to get
   back" is a question with a different answer for every character, and it drives
   decisions for the whole game. A shrunken backstory generates nothing.

## The question to ask a player

One question, and it does all of it:

> **What did the prologue take away from them that they have not replaced?**

Follow it with *"and what did they keep that they were not supposed to keep?"* -
which is where the one item, one contact or one piece of knowledge that survived
comes from. That single retained thing is usually the best detail in the whole
backstory, because it is the seam between who V was and who they are now.

## The failure mode

**Do not let the amputation become an excuse for helplessness.** V is not
diminished as a person; V is unequipped. The character should still *know* things
- how a corporate escalation works, which streets are watched, how to fix an
engine - and be visibly frustrated at not being able to act on it. That gap is
the character. Playing it as timidity throws away the whole device.
