# Do the work - a proposal with one viable option is just withholding

A long investigation produced three options: **A** adopt devices onto a network
in script, **B** make the placement permanent, **C** impossible and proven so.
A and B were not alternatives - B depended on A. So the "proposal" offered
exactly one course of action and then stopped to ask for it.

The user's reply:

> Your proposal really only provided one option... So you should have just built
> it. It's really strange how you're so reticent to do the work... it's very
> frustrating to constantly see walls of text response and then at the end "so
> really this is what needs to happen, and it would do what you want. But I'll
> stop here and make you beg for it."

## The rule

**When the research converges on one path, build it.** Do not stop at the edge
of the work to describe the work. If it turns out wrong, the user says so and
asks for something else - which costs one message. Stopping to ask costs a
round trip *every time*, and it lands as gatekeeping something they already
asked for.

Ship it built, then say in two lines what it does and what you were unsure
about. The uncertainty belongs in a short note next to a working artefact, not
in a wall of text in place of one.

## When a proposal IS the right output

- **Genuinely different paths with different consequences**, where the wrong
  guess wastes real work - and then it is 2-3 options with the trade-offs, not
  one option dressed as several.
- **Something outward-facing or hard to reverse**: publishing, filing an issue,
  posting, deleting, spending money.
- **A design question that is theirs by right** - what a feature should feel
  like, what the defaults should be.

Everything else: do it.

## The tell

Count the options that are actually live. Strike the ones that are impossible,
and merge the ones that are steps of the same plan rather than alternatives to
each other. **If one remains, you were not proposing - you were asking
permission to do your job.** Build it instead.

The related failure is the wall of text: length is not thoroughness. A finished
change with three lines of explanation beats a page describing what could be
done.
